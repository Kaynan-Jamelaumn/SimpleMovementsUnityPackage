# 04 — Plants

[← Agriculture](README.md)

## Planting by hand

A plant starts as **one seed in one hole**, and everything about how it was sown counts:

* **The soil's working** (*tilth*): seeds come up poorly in unworked, packed ground (each species has the working it
  needs); stones, packing and salt lower the chance too.
* **The depth**: every species has its best sowing depth and a tolerance; too shallow dries out, too deep never reaches
  the light. Surface-sown seeds (strawberries...) need light: leave them uncovered. A seed left uncovered when it should
  be covered dries out and is taken by birds.
* **The spacing and the rows**: roots share the soil — two plants too close compete for water and food, shade each
  other and catch each other's pests and diseases. Every species has the room it needs and its row spacing.
* **The time**: each species germinates only in its sowing seasons and warm enough (or cool enough) soil; some come up
  better sown at night, some under a new moon.
* **Water**: a seed needs moist soil to swell; waterlogged, cold soil rots it.
* **The seed itself**: its own vigour (from its genes).

## The stages

**Seed → Germination → Seedling → Juvenile → Vegetative → Flowering → Fruit development → Fruit maturation → Ready to
harvest → Overripe → Senescence → Dead.** Each species sets its days per stage in good conditions; the conditions decide
the real pace. Perennials go round from flowering to rest (dormancy) and back each year; single-harvest crops (roots,
cabbages) are done when harvested; annuals produce (possibly several flushes) and die at their season's end.

## Growth: the scarcest need decides

At every step a growing plant works out how well each need is met: **temperature, water, light, nutrients, pH, soil
type, salinity, packing, humidity, crowding, neighbours, damage, magic, health**. The scarcest decides most of its growth
(Liebig's law of the minimum), the others still count a little:

> growth = (scarcest)^0.6 × (geometric mean of all)^0.4

A plant short of nitrogen grows as slowly as one short of light, whatever else it has. The moon nudges the growth of
moon-bound species. The [trial plot](06-Authoring-Species-and-Materials.md#the-trial-plot) tells a designer which need
held a species back most.

## Health, stress and quality

* **Health** (0–1) falls with drought, waterlogging, frost, heat, sudden temperature changes, scorching light, salt,
  toxic excess, deep hunger, pests and diseases; it recovers slowly when nothing harms the plant. At 0 it dies. A
  resting perennial bears frost and heat far better; an established perennial (woody, deep-rooted) bears hunger, pests
  and disease far longer than a soft annual.
* **Stress** (0–1, about a day's memory) is how hard its life is now. Stressed plants attract more pests and catch
  more diseases.
* **Quality** comes from the plant's **whole history** — a weighted record of how well it lived through every hour,
  most of all while its produce grew — plus its genes. A plant that suffered a drought in flower gives poorer fruit
  even if it recovered. Every piece of produce has its own quality.

## Pests and diseases

Eight pests — **aphids, caterpillars, mites, beetles, sap-suckers, root parasites, slugs, snails** — and six diseases —
**fungal, root rot, wilt, leaf disease, bacterial, viral** — each with its weather (aphids in mild weather, mites in
hot dry air, slugs on wet nights, fungi on wet leaves, root rot in waterlogged soil with soil diseases), its liking for
each species and its damage. Colonies grow towards what the plant can feed and spread to neighbours; natural enemies
keep a healthy garden in check.

They are **visible**: insects on the stems and under the leaves (drawn on the plant up close), holes in leaves,
spots, mould, wilting. Controls: **pick them off** (tweezers help), **sprays** (herbal: mild; alchemical pesticide: kills
every insect, the helpful too), **fungicide**, **slug bait**, **ladybirds** (eat the soft pests for a while), **pruning**
out diseased parts, a **soil cleanser** for diseased soil, better **spacing** and air, and **companions** that repel
pests.

## Neighbours

Each species can act on the plants around it (*Companions*): help them grow, **repel** some pests, **attract
pollinators**, or **inhibit** them (allelopathy, a greedy competitor), within a radius — on one species, a whole
category, or every other plant. Taller neighbours shade shorter ones; close neighbours share root zones (and compete for
water and food); a **nitrogen fixer** (beans) feeds the soil around its roots.

## Produce

What a species gives (*Produce*): **fruit** (from pollinated flowers), **leaves** (cut as they come, some grow back),
**roots** (one per plant, pulled up), **flowers**, or **grain** (cut when dry). It may be food, medicinal, alchemical,
magical, a crafting material or an ornament.

**Every piece ripens on its own timer.** A flowering plant opens flowers over days (in flushes, each species with its
count and interval); each pollinated flower becomes a piece that goes through **Bud → Set → Early → Immature → Developing
→ Mature → Ripe → Overripe → Rotten** at its own pace (warmth, water, the plant's health), so one tomato plant carries
small green fruit, ripening fruit and a ripe one at once. A piece is best **picked ripe**: picked early it is poorer
(and some cannot be picked yet); left too long it goes overripe, rots and falls. **Pollination** is by the species'
way — itself, insects (warm, dry, calm weather; flowers nearby help), the wind, or only by hand with a brush — and an
unpollinated flower drops.

Harvesting gives items, each copy with its own **quality** (and **potency** for medicinal, alchemical and magical
produce, which the moon at picking time changes), sometimes a **rare lustre** from a rare gene.

## Dormancy, pruning and perennials

Perennials rest in their **dormancy seasons** (shedding their leaves, life drawn into the roots), gather **cold hours**
over winter (some flower only after enough), grow over years to full size and live their *Lifespan Years*. **Pruning**
— cut out dead wood or diseased parts, thin the crown (light and air inside: fewer diseases, better fruit), shape it,
cut it back hard (new growth), pinch out tips (herbs keep making leaves) — at the right season helps; at the wrong one,
or too hard, it wounds the plant and lets diseases in. Each species says whether and when it needs pruning.

## Seeds, genes and crosses

**Every seed is its own**: its genes travel with it as its per-copy code. Twelve traits, each from −2 to +2 (0 = the
species as it is in the wild):

| Trait | Effect |
|---|---|
| Growth speed, Maturation speed | How fast it grows / its produce ripens |
| Cold resistance, Heat resistance, Drought resistance | Widens what it bears |
| Disease resistance | Fewer pests and diseases |
| Fruit quantity, Fruit quality | Yield and quality |
| Medicinal potency, Alchemical concentration | Strength of what it gives |
| Max size, Nutrient efficiency | Size; less food needed |

plus the seed's **vigour** (its chance to come up) and, rarely, a **rare trait** (a lustre and a strength its kind does
not usually have). Bought or found seeds are wild.

* **Saving seeds** from ripe produce gives seeds of that plant crossed with whatever pollinated the flower: itself, or
  a neighbour of the same *Cross Group* (insects and wind carry pollen within a few metres; a brush carries it where
  you choose). Each trait comes from one parent or between them, sometimes a step up or down (mutation; the breeder's
  skill tilts it upwards).
* **Breeding**: choose the best plants for seed, year after year, and the traits climb. The genes are **never shown**:
  only what grows from them.
* **Hybrids**: some crosses between species of one Cross Group sometimes give seeds of another species (the built-in
  rule: a moonpetal crossed with a frostbloom sometimes gives twilight bloom seeds). Designers add their own ([chapter 06](06-Authoring-Species-and-Materials.md)).
